Tokenized Remittances: A Feasibility Framework for Pakistani Diaspora Investment

Fawad Liaqat First published March 2026 Last updated: November 2026 16 min read
Abstract

Pakistan received a record US$41.6 billion in workers' remittances in FY26, up 8.6% from US$38.3 billion in FY25 [1]. This analysis asks whether tokenization could channel a small share of those flows into regulated investment, and whether current SBP, SECP and PVARA rules allow it. Since this article was first written, the Virtual Assets Act 2026 has passed [7] and the SBP has allowed banks to serve licensed virtual asset service providers [9]. Even so, a tokenized remittance investment product does not appear feasible today: remittances must pass through authorized dealers, tokenized assets are not a recognised non resident investment category, and I found no framework for stablecoins. A sandbox pilot that uses existing foreign currency accounts, not stablecoins, is the most realistic first step. A simple sensitivity table shows what different redirection rates would mean in dollars; these are not forecasts.

Update note, November 2026: first published March 2026. This version corrects the FY25 remittance total (US$38.3 billion, not US$34.9 billion), moves to FY26 data, updates the virtual assets law, and removes a contradiction: the first version said stablecoins could not legally operate and then proposed a pilot settled in USDC and USDT. The market scenarios have been cut back to arithmetic that can be checked. Earlier text remains in the repository history.

1. Introduction

Workers' remittances reached US$41.6 billion in FY26 (July 2025 to June 2026) [1]. In June 2026 the largest sources were Saudi Arabia, the UAE, the UK and the USA [1]. I do not have a sourced breakdown of how recipients use this money, so this article does not claim that remittances are "overwhelmingly consumed", as the first version did.

Asset tokenization is being tested elsewhere: Singapore through Project Guardian [10; not re-checked], Dubai through its crypto token framework [11; not re-checked], and Switzerland through its DLT laws [12; 13; not re-checked]. The question is whether tokenization could channel a small share of Pakistan's remittances into productive investment, within Pakistan's rules. This article looks at the rules, at simple sizing arithmetic, and at a compliant technical design. The answer today is no, but the path is identifiable.

2. Regulatory Landscape

2.1 SBP Foreign Exchange Manual

The Foreign Exchange Manual sets permitted foreign exchange operations [2; not re-checked]. Inward remittances are processed through authorized dealers such as banks. I found no provision for an intermediary platform to take remittance funds before conversion to rupees. This is the most binding constraint.

Non residents can invest in government securities, sukuk and listed equities, and the Roshan Digital Account lets them hold foreign currency [2; not re-checked]. Tokenized assets are not recognised as an investment category under the manual as I read it.

Naya Pakistan Certificates, offered through Roshan Digital Accounts since 2020, show that diaspora demand for regulated Pakistani investment exists [3; not re-checked]. The first version quoted US$3.1 billion from about 500,000 accounts by the end of 2023; I have not re-checked those figures and do not use them in the sizing below. NPCs are government backed and SBP regulated, not run by an intermediary, which matters legally.

2.2 SECP sandbox, the Virtual Assets Act 2026 and the SBP's 2026 circular

The SECP runs a regulatory sandbox [4; not re-checked], and its second cohort in 2025 included real estate tokenization [5; not re-checked]. The SECP has also published a position paper on digital asset trading platforms [6; not re-checked].

The Pakistan Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority (PVARA) was first set up under a 2025 presidential ordinance. Parliament passed the Virtual Assets Act 2026 on 6 March 2026, giving PVARA powers over licensing of virtual asset service providers, market surveillance and anti money laundering compliance, with coordination with the SBP and SECP [7]. PVARA's mandate also covers supervising exchanges and consumer protection [8]. In April 2026 the SBP issued a circular that replaces its 2018 ban: banks may now open accounts for PVARA licensed providers and their customers, with client money held in segregated, rupee denominated, non remunerative accounts [9].

Stablecoins are a separate question. The reports I read on the Act and on the SBP circular do not describe any framework for stablecoins [7; 9]. The first version called this "regulatory limbo" and said stablecoins "cannot legally operate". I can only say that I found no rule authorising them. Either way, a pilot should not depend on them.

2.3 International precedents

Singapore: MAS's Project Guardian tests tokenization with industry under supervision [10; not re-checked]. The lesson is controlled experiments with clear metrics before commercial launch. The first version also cited a 2026 tokenized bills pilot and a fund that "reached $1.2 billion within six months (Q2 to Q3 2026)". The second claim described events after the article was written and has no source, so both are removed.

Dubai: the DFSA updated its crypto token framework in January 2026 [11; not re-checked]. The lesson is to regulate digital assets inside the existing regime rather than in a parallel one.

Switzerland: Swiss law treats many asset tokens under existing securities law, with DLT specific amendments [12; 13; not re-checked]. The lesson is technology neutral regulation. The first version called this "the most mature framework globally"; I cannot support that ranking.

The five largest corridors were said to be 78% of inflows, but its own corridor figures summed to about 81% of the total it used. 4. Technical Architecture

4.1 System design overview

The design below responds to the constraints in Section 2. It puts regulator visibility, investor identity checks at the protocol level and licensed custody first.

Blockchain layer: a permissioned or hybrid chain (for example Hyperledger Besu, or Polygon with permissioning) is more likely to satisfy a regulator than a public chain, because it allows identity checks, monitoring and intervention.

Token standard: ERC-1400 or ERC-3643 (T-REX) support transfer restrictions, investor allow lists and forced transfers for regulatory intervention. Plain ERC-20 does not.

KYC and AML: identity checks through NADRA for Pakistani nationals, enhanced due diligence for non residents in line with FATF standards, and on chain identity attestation tied to the allow list.

Custody and settlement: a licensed custodian holds the underlying assets (property titles, sukuk, fund units). Tokens map to beneficial ownership in the custodian's books.

Fiat on and off ramp: the binding constraint. An authorized dealer must accept funds and convert them, and the exit must also go through authorized channels [2; not re-checked]. Both would need explicit SBP permission that I found no evidence of.

4.2 Eligible asset classes

Four asset classes look most suitable for a first product. Real estate has the clearest local precedent, given the SECP sandbox's 2025 real estate tokenization approval [5; not re-checked]. Infrastructure assets offer long term cash flows. Sukuk fit the preferences of many diaspora investors. Managed funds spread risk across assets.

5. Feasibility and a Possible Path

5.1 Feasibility as of November 2026

Is a tokenized remittance investment product feasible today? Not as far as I can find. Three blockers remain: (1) remittances must go through authorized dealers, with no provision for an intermediary platform [2; not re-checked]; (2) I found no framework for stablecoins under the Virtual Assets Act or the SBP's 2026 circular [7; 9]; (3) tokenized assets are not a recognised non resident investment category [2; not re-checked].

What has changed since March 2026 is that the legal base for virtual asset providers now exists, and banks may serve licensed providers [7; 9]. That makes a supervised pilot more plausible than before. The first version said the IMF programme constraint "expires in 2027"; I have not verified that, so it is removed.

5.2 What would need to change

Four policy steps would be needed: (1) an SBP route for licensed firms to receive remittance funds for investment, with lock in periods and conversion caps; (2) a decision by the SBP and PVARA on whether, and how, stable value tokens may be used; (3) an SECP sandbox approval for a pilot; (4) recognition of tokenized securities as an eligible non resident investment.

The sandbox is the easiest entry point. A pilot should start with funds already held in Roshan Digital Accounts, invest in government backed assets such as sukuk, and convert to rupees only through authorized channels. It should not use USDC, USDT or any other stablecoin unless and until a rule allows it. The first version proposed holding USDC and USDT in escrow, which contradicted its own reading of the rules; that proposal is withdrawn.

5.3 An illustrative timeline

The phases and account targets below are my assumptions for discussion, not estimates from data.

Phase 1: sandbox application and approval; two asset classes (real estate and sukuk); a small, capped group of investors.

Phase 2: pilot with regular reporting to the SECP and SBP on investor behaviour, redemptions, custody and compliance; a third asset class if results allow.

Phase 3: rule changes based on pilot evidence, then a move from sandbox to licensing.

6. Conclusion

Remittances are large and growing: US$41.6 billion in FY26 [1]. Even 1% would be about US$0.42 billion a year (Table 1). The legal ground has shifted since March 2026, with a Virtual Assets Act and banking access for licensed providers [7; 9]. But the core blockers in the foreign exchange rules remain, and a pilot should be built on foreign currency accounts and government backed assets, not on stablecoins. The next step is evidence: a survey of diaspora investors and a small supervised pilot. I would welcome comments from people who know these rules from the inside.
