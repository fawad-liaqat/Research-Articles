The Behavioral Last Mile: Why Pakistan's Payment Rails Run Ahead of Its People

Fawad Liaqat First published March 2026 Last updated: November 2026 ~18 min read
Abstract

Background: In FY26 digital channels carried 92% of Pakistan's retail payments by count, and Raast moved 2,755 million transactions, more than twice the year before [1, p. 7; 1, p. 31]. By value, though, only 38% of retail payments were digital [1, p. 7], and only 27% of adults had an account in the latest global survey [2, p. 298]. The rails are built. Most people are not on them.

Methods: A comparative case study using the State Bank's Annual Payment Systems Review FY 2025-26, the World Bank's Global Findex 2025, press reports of the Karandaaz Financial Inclusion Survey 2024, and official releases on India's UPI. India and Kenya serve as comparison cases.

Results: The first barrier is access: most adults have no account, and among those who do, about 90% already pay digitally [2, p. 167]. Behind access sit three barriers that shape how much people use the rail once they are on it: trust (among excluded adults, 52% to 63% trust banks and mobile money services, against over 90% of the included [3, p. 27]), incentives (merchant payments are 6.6% of Raast volume, my calculation from [1, p. 31]), and design and literacy (a 65 point gender gap in phone ownership [2, p. 25]).

Conclusions: Five proposals, ordered access first, then use: target account opening among women and rural adults, publish and review merchant fees on small payments, build a basic phone route into Raast, pay agents for helping customers pay digitally, and set Urdu first design standards. These are proposals to test, not proven remedies.

Keywords: digital payments, financial inclusion, behavioral economics, Pakistan, Raast, cash persistence, UPI, M-Pesa

Update note, November 2026: this article was first published in March 2026 on FY25 data. It now uses the State Bank's FY26 review and Findex 2025. The earlier version's headline claim that "60% of the economy is cash" had no source and has been removed, and its argument has shifted from behaviour first to access first. Earlier text remains in the repository history.

1. Introduction

Pakistan's retail payments reached 14.3 billion transactions worth PKR 673 trillion in FY26 (July 2025 to June 2026). Digital channels carried more than 92% of them by count, up from 88% in FY25 and 85% in FY24 [1, p. 7]. Raast, the State Bank's instant payment system, launched its first phase in January 2021 [12]. In FY26 it moved 2,755.2 million transactions worth PKR 80.1 trillion, against 1,276.0 million in FY25 [1, p. 31]. QR enabled merchants rose from 1.1 million to 3.8 million in a year under a subsidy within the Prime Minister's Cashless Pakistan Initiative [1, p. 7]. The subsidy ran from 1 September 2025 to 30 June 2026 [4, p. 58]. On 13 November 2026 the government said it would continue subsidising merchant fees on Raast QR payments; the terms were not published [5].

The other side of the picture is just as clear. By value, the digital share was 38% [1, p. 7]. About PKR 415.7 trillion of PKR 672.7 trillion still moved over the counter at branches and agents, roughly 62% (my calculation) [1, p. 9]. Of 1,091 million ATM transactions, 1,064 million were cash withdrawals, or 97.6% (my calculation) [1, p. 26]. And only 27% of adults have an account; 25% made or received a digital payment [2, p. 298]. Pakistan's own survey puts inclusion higher, at 35% in 2024 [3, p. 7]. The two surveys use different samples and definitions; I use Findex for comparisons across countries.

The question is why a country where nine in ten retail payments are digital by count still runs much of its economy in cash. The first version of this article answered with behaviour: trust, incentives and literacy among people who could use the system but do not. The newer data points to a prior step. Findex reports that about 90% of Pakistani account owners already make or receive digital payments, on a base of very few account owners [2, p. 167]. So the first barrier is access. Behaviour matters once people are on the rail. I call the whole set of barriers the behavioral last mile.

2. Literature Review

Findex has tracked account ownership since 2011. Worldwide, 79% of adults had an account in 2024, up from 74% in 2021 [2, p. xxi]. Access and use are not the same thing: India has 89% account ownership but 48% digital payment use [2, p. 297].

The Technology Acceptance Model holds that people adopt a system they find useful and easy to use [13]. Later work adds trust as a key factor where institutions are not trusted [14]. Prospect theory explains why a familiar option such as cash can feel safer than a new one, because losses weigh more than equal gains [15]. Diffusion of innovations stresses compatibility with existing habits and how visible the benefits are [16]. Zucker's work on institutional trust explains why trust in a known person can stand in for trust in an institution [17]. These are lenses, not evidence about Pakistan.

3. Methodology and Data Sources

Pakistan is the main case. Payment data comes from the State Bank's Annual Payment Systems Review FY 2025-26, whose FY26 figures are provisional [1]. Account and usage data come from Findex 2025 [2]. Trust, gender and literacy figures for Pakistan come from the Karandaaz Financial Inclusion Survey 2024 narrative report [3]. The first November update cited press reports of that survey; where they differed from the report, the report is used.

India and Kenya are comparison cases. Their systems are at different ages and their fiscal years differ from Pakistan's, so the comparison is about scale and reach, not a controlled experiment. Figures I calculated are marked "my calculation" with their inputs. Page numbers are the printed pages of each document.

4. Pakistan's Digital Payment Landscape

Raast grew from 147.2 million transactions worth PKR 3.1 trillion in FY23 to 2,755.2 million worth PKR 80.1 trillion in FY26 [1, p. 31]. Person to person payments were 2,537.2 million of the FY26 total, and person to merchant payments rose from 4.9 million to 182.5 million [1, p. 31]. By June 2026 there were 49.2 million registered Raast P2P IDs and 3.6 million merchants on Raast's merchant alias [1, p. 11].

Around Raast, the network grew too. Branchless banking agents rose from 731,814 to 837,244; POS devices from 227,388 to 337,791, largely through Soft POS; mobile banking users from 24.1 million to 30.4 million; and branchless banking app users from 79.2 million to 99.1 million [1, p. 15; 1, p. 24]. Mobile phone channels together carried 11.1 billion transactions, up 79% [1, p. 7].

Count and value tell different stories. Mobile phone channels carried 78% of retail transactions by count but 23% of value, which the State Bank attributes to their low value, high frequency nature [1, p. 16]. Digital payments carry many small transactions. Larger payments still move mostly over the counter.

Merchant use is thin. Account based merchant payments were 13.0% of retail payments [1, p. 19], and person to merchant payments were 6.6% of Raast volume (my calculation) [1, p. 31]. The State Bank reports 297 million QR and till payments against 3.8 million QR merchants [1, p. 10], about 77 per merchant per year (my calculation). Many merchants joined partway through the year, so the rate for active merchants is higher.

5. Layer 0: Access

Before trust, incentives or design can matter, a person needs an account and a way to reach it. Only 27% of Pakistani adults have one [2, p. 298]. The gender gap in account ownership is 30 percentage points, four or more times the global gap [2, p. 98]. The Karandaaz survey reports 56% of men and 14% of women financially included [3, p. 7].

Phones are part of access. Pakistan has a 65 point gap between men and women in mobile phone ownership [2, p. 25]. Among Pakistani women without a phone, 40% name family disapproval as a reason; no men do [2, p. 32]. These are not problems a better app can fix on its own.

This is where India's experience is a caution rather than a model. India reached 89% account ownership, but only 48% of adults pay digitally [2, p. 297]. Pakistan has the opposite pattern: few accounts, but most account owners use them for digital payments [2, p. 167].

6. Layer 1: Trust

In the Karandaaz survey for 2024, trust is lower outside the system: 63% of financially excluded adults trust state owned banks, 54% private banks and 52% mobile money services, against 93%, 90% and 92% of the financially included [3, p. 27]. Among adults who borrowed, 85% borrowed from family and friends [3, p. 25]. The first November update quoted press figures (9% and 8% trust, 36% comfort) that do not appear in the survey report; they are withdrawn.

Informal credit runs on relationships built over years. Trust theory suggests that trust in a known person can substitute for trust in an institution [17]. Kenya's M-Pesa is often described as having grown this way, through shopkeepers who acted as agents [18]. I have not re-checked the history of Kenya's agent network against a primary source for this update, so I treat it as context, not evidence.

Pakistan has 837,244 branchless banking agents [1, p. 24]. Of the PKR 3.7 trillion they handled in FY26 [1, p. 26], about PKR 1.0 trillion was cash deposits and withdrawals [1, p. 11], roughly a quarter (my calculation). Whether agents could become the trusted bridge to Raast, which is reached through apps, is an open question.

7. Layer 2: Incentives

Merchants who accept cards pay a merchant discount rate, and digital payments leave a record. Cash has neither. The first version of this article quoted an MDR range of 1.5% to 2.5% and a worked cost example. I could not find a published source for that range, so both are removed. What the data does show is that incentives move merchants: QR merchants more than tripled within a year once a subsidy arrived [1, p. 7], a subsidy of 0.5% of value or PKR 100 per payment, whichever is lower, shared equally between the merchant's and the customer's financial institutions [4, p. 58], and Raast merchant payments rose from 4.9 million to 182.5 million [1, p. 31]. Use per merchant is still low (Section 4).

India made UPI and RuPay payments free of merchant discount rate from 1 January 2020 [9]. The first version said this applied below Rs 2,000 from 2019; that was wrong. UPI volume rose from about 46.0 billion transactions in FY 2021-22 to 241.6 billion in FY 2025-26 [7], and merchant payments are now 63% of UPI volume [8]. Many policies changed over those years, so this does not isolate the effect of zero MDR.

8. Layer 3: Design and Literacy

Pakistan's 2023 census puts literacy at 68.00% for men and 52.84% for women, and 41.67% for rural women [6]. The Karandaaz Digital Literacy Index scores men 65 and women 48, and urban adults 70 against rural 51 [3, p. 30]. On the survey's financial literacy measure (risk diversification, inflation, percentages and compound interest), 32% of men and 12% of women pass, as do 38% of the financially included against 12% of the excluded [3, p. 11]. This is a strong association with inclusion; it does not show that literacy causes it. Findex reports difficulty reading or typing as a common reason for not owning a phone in South Asia [2, p. 32].

Raast is reached through bank and wallet apps. For people with a basic phone, low literacy or limited English, that means more steps than cash. This is a hypothesis; I have not measured it. Pakistan does have 46.1 million registered call centre and IVR banking users [1, p. 9], so voice channels are familiar to banks. India launched UPI123Pay in 2022 so that feature phone users could make UPI payments [10].

Literacy is one enabler among several. The family disapproval finding in Section 5 shows that some barriers sit outside the person's own skills.

9. Conclusions and Proposals

Pakistan's payment rail is growing fast and is not the bottleneck. The binding constraint is access: most adults have no account, and the gap between men and women is wide. Trust, incentives and design then decide how much people use the rail once they are on it. These layers interact, but I have no data to show that they "multiply", as the first version claimed.

Five proposals follow, ordered access first, then use. Each is a proposal to test, not a proven remedy.

  1. Target account opening among women and rural adults. Use the agent network and biometric account opening. The 2026 Eid cattle market drive opened about 12,500 accounts alongside QR acceptance [1, p. 21].
  2. Publish and review merchant fees on small digital payments. Precedent: India's zero MDR on UPI and RuPay from 2020 [9].
  3. Build a basic phone route into Raast via USSD or IVR. Precedent: UPI123Pay [10]. In the Karandaaz survey 46% of women own a smartphone, against 82% of men [3, p. 29].
  4. Pay agents for helping customers make digital payments, not only for cash in and cash out. No verified precedent is cited here.
  5. Set Urdu first and voice first design standards for Raast enabled apps. No verified precedent is cited here.

The first version proposed a tax deduction for documented digital spending, citing Brazil's Pix and EU accessibility rules as precedents. I could not support either precedent, so that proposal is withdrawn until it can be.

I would welcome other readings of this data, especially from people who work with merchants or agents.
