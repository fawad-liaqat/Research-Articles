Agentic AI in Financial Compliance: Mapping the Regulatory Gap in Pakistan

First published March 2026 Last updated: November 2026
Abstract

Agentic AI systems that act on compliance decisions, rather than only recommending them, are entering financial services. This policy analysis asks how Pakistan's financial regulators address them. As of 17 November 2026 I found no published State Bank, SECP or PTA guidance specific to autonomous AI in compliance, fraud detection or transaction approval, although the State Bank said in 2025 that it was preparing AI guidelines and its governor has called for human oversight of AI decisions [19; 20]. Pakistan is compared with three models: the EU AI Act, whose high risk deadlines were pushed back by an omnibus amendment in July 2026 [2]; Singapore, where MAS issued final AI risk management guidelines in October 2026 [9]; and the UK, where the FCA relies on existing rules and published the Mills Review in July 2026 [15]. A gap matrix across five dimensions shows where Pakistan lacks rules, and the article ends with proposals for a phased approach.

Updated November 2026 with SBP FY26 data and new sources.

1. Introduction

A compliance officer at a Pakistani fintech receives an alert: the system has frozen a customer's account, flagged three transactions as suspicious and filed a regulatory report. No human reviewed the decision before it ran. The system is an agentic AI: it does not only predict or recommend, it perceives its environment, reasons about goals and acts.

The scenario is illustrative. The distinction it draws matters. A traditional model produces a risk score and a human decides. Rule based automation follows fixed logic. An agentic system interprets context, weighs goals, picks an action and carries it out. In compliance terms, it is not advising. It is deciding.

That raises questions existing rules were not written for. Who is liable when an autonomous system wrongly freezes a legitimate account? What explanation is owed when a machine made the decision? Can such a system meet requirements that assume a human decision maker?

This article maps the state of AI regulation in Pakistani financial services and compares it with the EU, Singapore and the UK across five dimensions, then proposes a path for Pakistan.

2. The Regulatory Landscape in Pakistan

The main finding is a negative one. As of 17 November 2026, I found no published guidance, circular or framework from a Pakistani financial regulator that addresses autonomous AI in compliance, fraud detection or transaction approval. Absence is hard to prove, so this rests on the searches described below and should be read as "not found", not "does not exist".

2.1 State Bank of Pakistan

A search of the SBP's banking circulars for "artificial intelligence", "machine learning", "autonomous", "algorithmic" and "agentic" found no circular on AI governance [16]. The SBP has said it is finalising guidelines on the responsible use of AI [17], as the press also reported in April 2025 [19]. I found no published version by November 2026. In January 2026 the governor said human oversight remains essential for AI driven decisions, especially loan approvals and customer due diligence [20]. The SBP's Cyber Shield strategy of February 2026 mentions AI only in passing [21], and its Technology Risk Management Framework for payment institutions (October 2025) covers technology and fraud controls but is not AI specific [18, p. 22].

Adoption is already under way. The SBP's Financial Stability Review 2024 reports a 2024 survey of 55 regulated entities in which about half had deployed AI in financial services or were developing it, for uses that include fraud detection and customer service [17].

2.2 Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan

In May 2025 the SECP released a concept paper on algorithmic trading for public comment [22]. Its scope is automated order execution in securities markets. It does not cover autonomous compliance systems, credit engines or fraud detection.

2.3 National AI Policy

The Federal Cabinet approved a National AI Policy in 2025 [25]. The policy provides for an AI council and a regulatory directorate [23]. I found no sector specific rules for financial services issued under it by November 2026. The PTA has not published AI guidance relevant to financial services that I could find.

The result is that Pakistani fintechs using agentic AI in compliance work under general rules that assume a human decision maker.

3. International Frameworks

Three jurisdictions take different approaches. Each is read against five dimensions: risk classification, explainability, liability, auditability and human oversight.

3.1 European Union: the AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689)

The EU AI Act classifies AI systems by risk and sets graduated duties [1]. For finance, Annex III lists AI used to assess creditworthiness as high risk, with the obligations in Articles 9 to 15. AI used to detect financial fraud is carved out of that credit category [1, Annex III], though it remains subject to the Act's general rules.

Article 14 requires high risk systems to allow effective human oversight: overseers should understand the system's limits, watch for automation bias, interpret outputs, override or reverse decisions, and be able to interrupt the system through a stop procedure [1]. Article 13 requires enough transparency for deployers to interpret outputs, and Article 12 requires automatic event logging [1].

Timeline. High risk obligations were due from 2 August 2026, but that date has been moved. The AI Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, entered into force on 27 July 2026. High risk obligations for Annex III systems now apply from 2 December 2027, and for Annex I products from 2 August 2028; most Article 50 transparency duties still applied from 2 August 2026 [24; 2].

Liability follows a provider and deployer model. Providers carry duties for risk management, data governance and conformity assessment. Deployers, such as banks using a system, must ensure human oversight and keep logs (Articles 25 and 26) [1].

The Act does not use the term "agentic AI". Under the Act, risk class depends on the use case listed in Annex III, not on how autonomous the system is. An agentic system used for credit scoring is high risk; one used for fraud detection is not, on that ground alone. The Future Society maps how the Act's existing provisions apply to AI agents across the provider and deployer chain [3].

3.2 Singapore: from FEAT to final AI risk management guidelines

MAS's FEAT principles (2018) set a principles based foundation [4]. The Veritas initiative produced assessment methods and toolkits [5], Project MindForge extended the work to generative AI [6], and a December 2024 information paper set expectations for AI model risk management [7].

MAS consulted on AI risk management guidelines in November 2025 [8] and issued the final guidelines on 7 October 2026. They apply to all financial institutions and all forms of AI, scaled to the risk materiality of each use. Boards and senior management are expected to oversee AI risk; institutions should keep AI inventories, assess risk and apply proportionate controls such as testing, human oversight and monitoring, and remain accountable for third party AI. The guidelines take effect on 7 October 2027, with some sections due by 7 October 2028 [9].

On agentic AI specifically, MAS notes the growing use of systems that act autonomously and use tools, asks institutions to review controls as this use grows, and plans to consult on more agentic AI guidance in 2027 [9].

3.3 United Kingdom: principles based supervision

The FCA's position, set out with the Bank of England and PRA in DP5/22 [10] and the feedback statement FS23/6 [11], is that existing frameworks can govern AI if firms apply them well. AI oversight sits within the Senior Managers and Certification Regime [12], the Consumer Duty and operational resilience rules [13]. Under SM&CR, senior managers remain accountable for outcomes in their area, including those produced by AI.

The FCA's 2024 AI Update confirmed this approach and said its framework does not specifically address the transparency or explainability of AI systems [13]. The Bank of England and FCA survey published in October 2026 found that 86% of responding firms use AI, up from 75% in 2024 [14].

The Mills Review was published on 6 July 2026. It makes seven priority recommendations for the FCA Board. It expects AI to recommend actions, initiate transactions and execute decisions within agreed limits, and two of its recommendations address agentic AI directly: enabling the foundations for agentic finance, and building an agentic supervisory model [15]. The recommendations are for the FCA Board to consider, so it is not yet clear whether the FCA will add AI specific rules.

Pakistan's column is mostly "not found". The three comparators each have something in place or in train: the EU through law, Singapore through supervisory guidelines, the UK through existing accountability rules and a fresh review. The gap is real, but it is a gap in published rules, which is the only thing I could check.

5. Proposals for the SBP

Pakistan should not copy any single model. Each reflects its own institutions and market. Pakistan's constraints include supervisory capacity and a young fintech sector.

5.1 Start with principles, then tools, then guidelines

Singapore's sequence is the most useful model: principles in 2018, tools and information papers next, final guidelines in 2026 [4; 9]. The SBP could begin with a circular on core principles (accountability, transparency, fairness, human oversight), which sets expectations without needing new enforcement machinery.

5.2 Require classification by autonomy and impact

Institutions should classify their AI systems by how autonomous they are and how much their decisions affect customers or regulatory duties. Systems that act on their own in high impact areas should face the strictest controls, in line with MAS's materiality approach.

5.3 Define who is liable

The deploying institution should bear primary responsibility to customers and to the SBP for AI outcomes, whether the system was built in house or bought. Liability with vendors can be settled by contract. This mirrors the EU deployer model, the UK's SM&CR and MAS's stance on third party AI.

5.4 Set minimum audit trails

Any AI system taking compliance decisions should log what it decided, on what data and with which model version. This is far short of the EU's Article 12, but a large step from no requirement at all. It should be designed with industry so it is feasible.

5.5 Set minimum human oversight

Oversight should scale with risk. Low value fraud screening may need only periodic review. Filing regulatory reports or freezing accounts should need human confirmation before the action runs. This is consistent with the governor's own statement on human oversight [20].

6. Conclusion

Since this article was first published, the three comparators have moved: the EU delayed its high risk deadlines, Singapore finalised its guidelines, and the UK published the Mills Review. Pakistan has signalled intent but, as far as I could find, has not published rules. The SBP can act now with clear principles, defined accountability and a roadmap, without waiting for full legislation. I would welcome corrections from anyone who knows of guidance I missed.
