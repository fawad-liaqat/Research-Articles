Why CBDCs Fail at the Interface: Design Lessons from the eNaira and Sand Dollar for Pakistan's Digital Rupee

First published March 2026 Last updated: November 2026
Abstract

Nigeria's eNaira saw very low use: an IMF review found that about 98.5% of wallets were unused in any given week [2]. The Bahamas' Sand Dollar has also struggled to spread. This article reads both through five design dimensions: onboarding, language, offline use, error handling, and fit with existing habits. It then maps each failure to a requirement for a Pakistani digital rupee, using census literacy data, State Bank payment data and Karandaaz survey figures. Pakistan's pilot was announced in 2025, but I could not confirm that it is live as of 17 November 2026.

Updated November 2026 with SBP FY26 data and new sources.

Introduction

Ninety eight point five percent. That is the share of eNaira wallets that were unused in any given week, according to an IMF review of the eNaira's first year published in May 2023 [1; 2]. The IMF described adoption as low and noted that it had not moved past an initial limited wave [1].

This article argues that the eNaira's weak uptake and the Sand Dollar's slow growth in the Bahamas are, in large part, design failures: onboarding friction, language barriers, limited offline use, opaque error handling, and a product that sat apart from the habits of its users. The pattern matters for Pakistan. In July 2025 an SBP spokesperson said the bank hoped to complete a digital currency pilot within the fiscal year ending June 2026 [17]. In August 2025 Dawn, citing Nikkei Asia, reported that Japan's Soramitsu would work with the SBP on the pilot [18]. In January 2026 the pilot was still described as planned [19], and the State Bank's FY26 payment systems review does not mention it [14]. As of 17 November 2026 I could not confirm from an official source whether the pilot has started.

Methodology Note

The audit relies on published evidence. Neither the Central Bank of Nigeria nor the Central Bank of The Bahamas publishes its internal design documents. The evidence base is the IMF review of the eNaira [1; 2]; academic analysis of eNaira app reviews [7; 8]; BIS publications [3; 4; 5]; Central Bank of Nigeria and Central Bank of The Bahamas documents [6; 9]; journalism and third party analysis [10; 11; 12; 20]; and Pakistani data from the census [13], the State Bank [14], the Karandaaz survey [15] and the PTA [16].

The Nigeria and Bahamas figures describe 2021 to 2024 and are kept as historical evidence. The Sand Dollar evidence is thinner than the eNaira's. Where evidence is missing, the audit says so.

The eNaira Audit

Onboarding friction

Onboarding friction was the eNaira's most immediate problem. The Speed Wallet app required email verification, BVN or NIN authentication, and acceptance of terms before any transaction. Analysis of app reviews found onboarding to be the most frequent complaint: email verification locked users out, BVN checks returned errors for valid details, and NIN verification was unreliable [7; 8]. The app was reported to have been pulled from Google Play shortly after launch with a low rating, and NIN verification problems were reported to have disconnected millions of mobile subscribers [11; 12]. The Central Bank of Nigeria designed a Tier 0 wallet, phone only with a low daily limit, to avoid identity checks [6], but user reports suggest NIN or BVN was still required in practice [7].

Language and localization

Language added to the problem. The eNaira app was available in English only, in a country where Hausa, Yoruba and Igbo are widely spoken, and its interface relied on text more than icons [7; 12]. An inclusion product offered only in English, to people who mostly speak other languages, misses its target.

Offline capability

Offline use was limited. The USSD code *997# gave feature phone access, but USSD still needs a cellular signal [6]. No stored value or NFC method was deployed. Where coverage or power failed, the product was not available.

Error handling

Error handling hurt trust. App review analysis found transaction failures with no explanation, registration errors that blocked retries, long support waits, and no confirmation that funds were safe after a failed transaction [7; 8]. For people who already doubted digital finance, one unexplained error could be enough to stop.

Integration with existing habits

Fit with existing habits was weak. The eNaira asked users to download a separate app, open a new account and verify identity again, to reach a payment channel that competed with apps they already used. In one survey, 49% of participants said they would not use the eNaira because existing options were enough [10].

The Sand Dollar Audit

The Sand Dollar has low friction for consumers: its lowest wallet tier requires no identity documents, within a small balance and monthly limit [9]. Merchant onboarding was weaker. By May 2023 there were about 1,500 merchant wallets against about 104,664 consumer wallets, merchant accounts were not linked to the banking system, and banks and credit unions were slow to sign on [20]. The result was easy entry with too few places to pay.

The Sand Dollar's offline design stands out. Value can be used without a network connection and reconciled later, a feature built with hurricane outages in mind [9].

Language is not a barrier in the Bahamas, and evidence on error handling is too thin to assess.

Pakistan's Demographic Context

The census counted 171.7 million people aged 10 and above, of whom 104.1 million can read. The literacy rate is 60.65% overall, 68.00% for men, 52.84% for women, 51.56% in rural areas and 41.67% for rural women [13]. That leaves about 67.6 million people aged 10 and above who cannot read (my calculation from the same table). About 60% of people aged 10 and above live in rural areas (my calculation, 102.5 of 171.7 million) [13].

The Karandaaz survey for 2024 puts financial inclusion at 35%, with 56% of men and 14% of women included [15, p. 7]. Trust is lower outside the system: 63% of financially excluded adults trust state owned banks and 52% trust mobile money services, against 93% and 92% of the included [15, p. 27]. Women are also less likely to own a mobile phone (46% against 82% of men) [15, p. 28]; the report prints identical figures for smartphone ownership on the next page, so which measure they describe is unclear [15, p. 29], and score lower on the survey's Digital Literacy Index (48 against 65) [15, p. 30].

The PTA's annual report for 2024-25 put telecom subscriptions above 200 million and telecom coverage above 92% [16; 21].

The State Bank's FY26 review counts 837,244 branchless banking agents, 99.1 million registered branchless banking app users and 7.9 million e-money wallets [14, p. 9]. The review does not report how many agents are active.

The hardest case for adoption is a rural woman. If she is aged 10 or above, her community's literacy rate is 41.67% [13]. Nationally, 14% of women are financially included [15, p. 7]. These are population rates, not her personal probabilities, and they cannot be multiplied together as if independent. Every design requirement below should still be tested against her.

Onboarding

Onboarding for the lowest tier should not depend on a verification step that fails in rural areas, which is what undid Nigeria's Tier 0 in practice. It should work by USSD or SMS on a basic phone, with a small daily limit. Moving up to a verified tier should be rewarded, not forced.

Language and localization

Language support should cover Urdu plus at least Punjabi, Sindhi and Pashto at launch. Every critical action (send, receive, check balance) should be possible by icon alone, because about 67.6 million people aged 10 and above cannot read [13].

Offline capability

Offline use should go further than the Sand Dollar. Agents, not only ATMs, should be loading points, and the mechanism should work on basic phones with a clear confirmation once the device reconnects. The offline limit should be set from evidence on household spending, which I have not analysed here.

Error handling

Error handling should treat the first transaction as the product's only chance. Every failure should say why in the user's language, confirm that funds are safe and offer a one tap retry. No user should be blocked for good after a failed check. Agents could be the first line of support.

Integration with existing habits

Fit with habits matters most. A digital rupee should not launch as a separate app. It should sit inside the wallets and bank apps people already use; branchless banking apps alone have 99.1 million registered users [14, p. 9]. Raast has 3.6 million merchants on its merchant alias [14, p. 11] and has processed about 4.7 billion transactions from FY22 to FY26 (my calculation from Table A-15) [14, p. 31]; that network could accept it. It should also offer something existing mobile money cannot, such as programmable government transfers, or it has no reason to exist.

Conclusion

The eNaira and the Sand Dollar teach the same lesson through different failures. Nigeria's design was inclusive on paper but hard to use in practice. The Bahamas made entry easy but gave users few places to pay. Both put the monetary design ahead of the user.

Pakistan would start with real assets: a large base of wallet users, 837,244 agents, and a fast growing Raast network [14, p. 9; 14, p. 31]. The open question is whether a digital rupee, if the pilot goes ahead, would be designed for people who do not yet trust financial institutions. The evidence from these two cases points one way: build into existing apps, work offline and on basic phones, and make onboarding forgiving. I would welcome views from anyone who has worked on these systems.
